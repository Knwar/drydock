# Craft checklists by edit level

Work top-down. Each level assumes the levels above it are stable; if the developmental pass finds a structural problem, keep line and copy notes to recurring patterns only.

## Developmental (structure, argument or story, audience fit)

- **Promise**: what does the opening (title, first paragraph, cold open, hook) teach the reader to expect? Does the rest pay that promise, or drift toward a different piece?
- **Purpose**: can you state what the piece is for in one sentence? Does that match the brief?
- **Audience fit**: assumed knowledge, jargon, examples, and tone match the stated audience. Nothing the audience needs is missing; nothing they already know is over-explained.
- **Argument (non-fiction)**: one thesis; every section advances it; claims are supported in proportion to their strength; counter-arguments acknowledged where the audience would raise them; the conclusion follows from what came before rather than restating the intro.
- **Story (fiction, narrative non-fiction, narrative audio)**: events cause or complicate each other (could the scenes be reordered without breaking anything? then it's a sequence, not a plot); each scene changes something; stakes are clear and specific to the character; information is withheld and revealed deliberately.
- **Shape and pacing**: where does pressure rise, plateau, rush, or sag? Is any section doing nothing that another section doesn't already do?
- **Length**: within the brief; the word count spent per section matches its importance.
- **Ending**: earns its conclusion; any call to action is the one the brief asked for.

Developmental notes point to a structural action: cut, move, expand, combine, split, reframe, seed earlier, delay a payoff. Prettier sentences don't fix a missing turn.

## Line (clarity, voice, rhythm)

- **Clarity**: can the reader follow every referent, step, and claim on one read? Confusion is not depth.
- **Voice**: does the language belong to this author, brand, narrator, or speaker, or has it drifted to competent-generic?
- **Rhythm**: sentence and paragraph length vary with the pressure of the moment; no monotone stretch.
- **Redundancy**: the text makes a point, then a sentence explains the point it just made. Cut the explanation.
- **Specificity**: one precise example or detail beats three adequate ones.
- **Openings and transitions**: paragraphs start with substance, not throat-clearing; transitions carry logic, not filler ("Moreover").
- **For the ear (scripts)**: one idea per sentence, no nested clauses, numbers rounded and spoken naturally, no lists longer than three spoken items.

Name patterns, not instances. Preserve deliberate roughness, fragments, dialect, and repetition when they serve the voice; if unsure whether something is a choice, query it.

## Copy (consistency, facts, names, formatting)

- Spelling, grammar, punctuation, usage, tense and person consistency.
- Names of people, companies, products, places: spelled and capitalized the same everywhere, and correct against a source.
- Numbers: consistent style (numerals vs words, units, currency), totals that add up, percentages that make sense.
- Dates and times: correct day/date pairs, time zones, "next week" style relative dates that will be wrong when published.
- Quotes: exact wording, attributed, sourced. A quote of a real person with no source is a must-fix.
- Links and references mentioned in the text: present, and pointing at what the text says they point at (never fill in a missing link yourself).
- Formatting: headings, lists, emphasis, captions, and alt text follow the destination's conventions and the brief.
- Terms: one name per concept; defined on first use if the audience needs it.

Fix silently only what is unambiguous and preserves meaning; query everything else. Group recurring errors as patterns.

## Fact and claim types to catch

- Statistics and numbers, especially round or dramatic ones.
- "Studies show", "experts say", "research proves" with no named source.
- Superlatives and firsts ("the largest", "the first", "the only").
- Causal claims ("X causes Y", "this led to").
- Quotes and paraphrases of real people.
- Claims about competitors, products, laws, prices, or dates that change over time.
- Health, legal, and financial statements presented as advice.
