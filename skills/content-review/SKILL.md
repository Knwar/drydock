# Content Review

Quality control for written and spoken work: judge a piece against what it was supposed to do, then against the craft standard for its form. The output is a verdict the author can act on, not an opinion.

## When to use

- A draft is done and needs a pass/fail before it moves on (chapter, article, essay, newsletter issue, social post or thread, video or podcast script, episode outline, landing copy).
- A revised draft comes back and the earlier notes need checking.
- Someone asks "is this ready?", "review this", "QC this", "does this meet the brief?".

Inputs to have before starting:

- The brief or request the piece answers, and any acceptance criteria.
- The style guide, brand voice notes, or earlier pieces in the series, if they exist.
- Sources the author used, for the fact check.
- For a revision: the previous review.

Not for rewriting the piece. Diagnose and recommend; produce replacement text only for short illustrative fixes or when explicitly asked.

## Core principles

1. **The brief is the contract.** Read it before the piece. A beautiful draft that misses the brief fails; a plain draft that meets every criterion and has no blocking craft issue passes.
2. **Evidence or it didn't happen.** Every judgment about the piece quotes the passage (or gives the timestamp, slide, or section) it rests on. "The intro is weak" is not a note. "The intro spends 140 words on background before the claim in paragraph 3 ('We think...')" is.
3. **Big before small.** Structure and argument problems make line edits moot. Order notes by what costs the reader most, and don't lead with commas when the arc is broken.
4. **Protect the author's voice.** The goal is the best version of this piece by this author, not the piece you would have written. Distinguish voice from error; when unsure, query instead of correcting.
5. **Never invent.** Don't invent sources, statistics, quotes, or corrections of fact. If you can't verify a claim, flag it as unverified. That is the finding.
6. **Separate must-fix from nice-to-have.** The verdict depends only on must-fix items. Taste goes in nice-to-have, labeled as taste.

## Method

1. **Read the brief.** Extract the acceptance criteria as a numbered list: purpose, audience, format, length, required points, tone, deadline-sensitive facts, calls to action, constraints (what must not appear). If there is no written brief, reconstruct one from the request and state it at the top of the review as an assumption. If the criteria are contradictory or missing something decisive (who is the audience?), say so instead of guessing.
2. **Read the whole piece once without notes.** Track the felt experience: where attention held, where it drifted, where you got lost. Many apparent problems resolve later in the piece; many real ones only show in retrospect.
3. **Check each criterion with evidence.** For every criterion: met / partly met / not met, plus the quote or locator that proves it. A criterion with no evidence either way is "not met" until shown otherwise.
4. **Developmental pass** (structure, argument or story, audience fit). See `references/craft-checklists.md`.
5. **Line pass** (clarity, voice, rhythm). Name patterns, not every instance: three or four recurring patterns with two or three examples each.
6. **Copy pass** (consistency, facts, names, numbers, formatting). Build a small style sheet as you go: names, spellings, capitalization, number style, terms.
7. **Fact and claim check.** List every checkable claim: numbers, dates, names, quotes, attributions, "studies show", superlatives, causal claims. Mark each verified (with the source you checked), unverified, or contradicted. Never supply a source you have not seen; an unverified claim gets "needs a source" or "soften or cut", not an invented citation.
8. **AI-writing tells.** Scan for machine-sounding prose using the avoid-ai-writing skill in detect mode, with the context profile that matches the piece (fiction for stories and dialogue). Report clear problems; label judgment calls as such. These are writing-quality notes, never an authorship verdict.
9. **Decide the verdict and write the report** in the format in `references/review-report.md`.

## Verdict rules

- **Pass**: every acceptance criterion met, no must-fix items. Nice-to-haves may remain.
- **Fail**: any criterion not met or partly met, any factual error, any unverified claim the piece relies on, any legal or safety risk (defamation, a quote attributed to a real person with no source, medical/financial advice stated as fact), or a structural problem that stops the piece doing its job.

Must-fix is anything that breaks the brief, misleads the reader, or embarrasses the author in public. Everything else is nice-to-have.

## What a good note looks like

Each note has four parts:

- **Where**: quote, paragraph number, timestamp, slide, or section heading.
- **What**: the problem, named precisely.
- **Why it matters**: the cost to the reader or to the brief.
- **Direction**: the kind of move that fixes it (cut, move, expand, combine, split, reframe, seed earlier, add a source, soften the claim, change the example), not a rewrite in your voice.

Bad: "Flow could be better." Good: "Para 4 to 5: the jump from pricing to hiring has no bridge, so the reader loses the argument's thread. Add one sentence tying hiring cost to the pricing point, or move para 5 after para 7 where hiring comes up again."

Query instead of directing when the change could alter meaning, voice, or a deliberate choice: "Did you intend X? A reader may read it as Y."

## Form-specific checks

Apply the general checklists, plus the form's own bar:

- **Chapter / fiction**: scene function (something changes), POV control, continuity against earlier chapters, chapter ending pull, dialogue subtext. The writing-craft and story-planning skills hold the deeper craft checks.
- **Article / essay / newsletter**: one clear thesis stated early, each section advances it, evidence matches the strength of each claim, headline and subhead promise what the body delivers, ending earns its conclusion.
- **Script (video, podcast)**: written for the ear (short sentences, no unreadable parentheticals, numbers spoken naturally), a hook in the first 15 to 30 seconds, segment transitions, runtime matches the word count at the speaker's pace.
- **Episode outline**: central question, segment order with timings, guest questions that ask for stories, cold open candidate, clear ending. See the podcast-production and show-notes skills.
- **Social post or thread**: first line works alone, one idea per post, platform length and format, a call to action only if the brief asks for one, nothing claimed that the linked piece doesn't support.

## Worked example (abridged)

Brief: "800 to 1,000-word newsletter essay for first-time freelancers on setting their first rate. Must include one worked pricing example, link to last month's issue on contracts, end with a reply-to-this-email question. No tool recommendations."

Extracted criteria:

1. Audience: first-time freelancers.
2. Length 800 to 1,000 words.
3. Topic: setting a first rate.
4. One worked pricing example.
5. Link to last month's contracts issue.
6. Ends with a reply prompt.
7. No tool recommendations.

Review excerpt:

| # | Status | Evidence |
|---|--------|----------|
| 2 | not met | 1,240 words |
| 4 | met | "Say you want $60k a year and can bill 1,000 hours..." (para 6) |
| 5 | not met | Contracts issue mentioned in para 9 ("as I wrote last month") with no link |
| 7 | not met | "I use Bonsai for invoices" (para 11) |

Must-fix: cut about 250 words (paras 2 to 3 restate the intro; para 11 goes with the tool mention); add the contracts link; remove the tool recommendation. Fact check: "most freelancers undercharge by 30%" (para 4) is unverified: needs a source or soften to the author's own experience. Verdict: FAIL.

## Common failure modes

- Reviewing against your taste instead of the brief.
- Notes with no quote or locator, so the author can't find or verify them.
- Fifty line notes on a piece that needs restructuring.
- Rewriting passages in your own voice and calling it a review.
- Passing a piece with an unverified statistic because it "sounds right".
- Inventing a source or "correct" figure to fix a claim.
- Calling the author's deliberate style (fragments, dialect, unusual rhythm) an error.
- Mixing must-fix and nice-to-have so the verdict is unclear.
- Praise padding, or no mention at all of what works.
- Re-reviewing a revision from scratch instead of checking each earlier must-fix first, then reading for new problems.

## Done means

- The brief's criteria are listed and each has a status with evidence.
- The verdict is one word, and follows mechanically from the must-fix list.
- Every must-fix is specific enough that the author could fix it without asking a follow-up question.
- Every factual claim the piece relies on is marked verified, unverified, or contradicted, with no invented sources.
- One or two sentences say what works and why, so the author knows which instincts to keep.
- The report fits on a page or two for a short piece; length scales with the piece, not with the reviewer's enthusiasm.
