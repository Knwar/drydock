# Editorial Calendar

Plan a publishing schedule that a solo creator or small team will actually keep. Works the same for a book (chapter schedule), a newsletter, a podcast, a blog, or social channels; the unit changes, the method doesn't.

## When to use

- Starting a new publication, show, book, or channel and deciding what goes out when.
- Turning a list of ideas into dated slots.
- Re-planning after a missed week, a launch date change, or a cadence that isn't working.
- Planning a season (podcast), a quarter (newsletter, social), or a manuscript (book).

Inputs to gather: the goal, the audience, the channels, the hours available per week, any fixed dates (launch, deadline, season start), past publishing history if any, and the existing idea list.

## Core principles

1. **Cadence is a promise; set one you can keep.** Base it on the real hours per week available and the measured or estimated hours per piece. A weekly show that slips to "whenever" does more damage than a biweekly show that never misses.
2. **Every publish date implies earlier dates.** A piece is research → draft → edit → publish-ready → published. Schedule the upstream steps backward from the publish date, or the calendar is a wish list.
3. **Buffer before launch.** Have pieces finished ahead of their slot before the first one goes out, and protect the buffer. When the buffer drops below the minimum, slow the cadence or batch, don't skip silently.
4. **Pillars keep the plan coherent.** Three to five recurring themes the audience comes for. Every slot belongs to a pillar; ideas that fit none go to a parking list.
5. **Make once, publish many.** Plan the flagship piece and its derivatives together, at creation time, not after.
6. **Consistency beats volume.** A smaller output that never misses builds more trust than bursts followed by silence.
7. **The calendar is reviewed, not admired.** It changes on a fixed review rhythm based on what shipped and what worked.

## Method

1. **Define the publication.** Audience, promise (what they get each time), format and typical length or runtime, channels, and the goal (readers, subscribers, listeners, sales, finishing the book). For a book: target word count, chapter count, and the deadline (submission, launch, or self-imposed).
2. **Measure capacity.** Hours per week available; hours per piece by stage (research, draft, edit, production, publishing). Use the person's past numbers if they have them; otherwise estimate, label the estimate, and plan to correct it after the first two or three pieces. Cadence = capacity ÷ hours per piece, rounded down, with slack.
3. **Choose pillars.** Three to five themes. Sources: what the audience asks about, what the person knows or has unique access to, what the goal needs, and gaps in what comparable publications cover. Give each pillar a one-line description and a target share of slots.
4. **Collect and score ideas.** List candidate pieces per pillar. Score them (see `references/calendar-templates.md` for the scoring table) and rank. The top of each pillar fills the first slots.
5. **Map key dates.** Holidays, seasonal moments, industry events, launches, the person's own commitments (travel, busy seasons), and platform quiet periods. Mark dates to aim for and dates to avoid. For each date-bound piece, note the latest date it is still useful.
6. **Lay out slots.** Fixed publish days per channel. Assign pieces to slots: alternate pillars, put evergreen pieces where nothing date-bound fits, and leave one open slot per four to six weeks for timely pieces.
7. **Back-schedule each piece.** From the publish date: publish-ready date (buffer before publish), edit window, draft window, research window, plus any external dependency (guest booked, interview recorded, data received, artwork, approvals). A piece whose research date is already past gets moved or simplified now, not on the day.
8. **Add repurposing chains.** For each flagship, list the derivatives and their slots (see below).
9. **Set the buffer rule and the review rhythm.** Write both down in the calendar itself.
10. **Sanity-check the load.** Count the hours per week the calendar implies, stage by stage. If any week exceeds capacity, move pieces, cut derivatives, or reduce cadence.

## Dependencies by format

- **Book**: outline → chapter draft → self-edit → (beta/editor feedback) → revision. Schedule chapters by word count per week; leave a full revision pass at the end, and plan structural revision before line edits. Later chapters that depend on decisions in earlier ones come after them.
- **Newsletter**: topic → research → draft → edit → design/format → scheduled send. Sponsors or partner mentions add an approval date.
- **Podcast**: topic → guest outreach and booking (often the longest lead time) → research and question prep → record → edit → show notes and chapter markers → publish → clips and posts. Book guests several episodes ahead and keep at least one solo or evergreen episode in reserve for cancellations.
- **Blog / articles**: topic → research → outline → draft → edit → visuals → publish → distribution posts.
- **Social**: derivatives of the above, plus native pieces. Batch-produce weekly; keep a small bank of evergreen posts for gaps.

## Repurposing chains

Plan derivatives at creation time so the flagship is built with liftable parts (stand-alone subheads, quotable lines, clear segments):

- **Podcast episode** → show notes, 2 to 4 short clips, quote cards, a newsletter section, a blog recap, social posts per key point.
- **Long article or essay** → newsletter version, a thread or carousel per section, a short video script, quote posts.
- **Book chapter** → excerpt or serialized newsletter issue, essay adaptation, social posts on its themes (watch publisher rights before excerpting).
- **Newsletter issue** → blog archive post, social thread, podcast segment.

Rules: each derivative is adapted to its channel, not pasted; space derivatives over days or weeks rather than the same hour; one flagship with good distribution beats several thin pieces.

## Calendar format

Use the calendar table in `references/calendar-templates.md`. Minimum columns: publish date, channel, pillar, working title, format, status, owner, and the research/draft/edit/ready dates. Keep one row per piece, including each derivative.

Status values: idea → scheduled → researching → drafting → editing → ready → published (→ repurposed). "Ready" means fully finished and sitting in the buffer.

## Worked example: capacity to cadence

A solo creator has 6 hours a week for a newsletter plus social.

| Stage | Hours per issue |
|-------|-----------------|
| Research | 1.5 |
| Draft | 2 |
| Edit and format | 1 |
| Three social derivatives | 1 |
| **Total** | **5.5** |

Leave about a fifth of the 6 hours unplanned and roughly 4.8 hours remain: less than one issue a week. Options: publish biweekly (2.75 hours a week, room for a buffer and an occasional extra piece), cut derivatives to one (4.5 hours, weekly becomes possible but tight), or batch two issues in one heavy week each month. Recommend biweekly with a two-issue buffer before launch, and re-measure after three issues.

The same arithmetic works for a book: 60,000 words remaining at 1,500 words a week is 40 weeks of drafting before any revision time. If the deadline is 30 weeks out, change the scope, the hours, or the deadline now.

## When the plan breaks

- **A piece misses its draft date**: swap in the next ready piece from the buffer and move the late piece back; never compress the edit window to keep the slot.
- **The buffer is empty**: drop derivatives first, then publish an evergreen piece or a lighter format (a short note, a Q&A, a replay), then reduce cadence for a defined period and tell the audience if the gap will be visible.
- **A timely opportunity appears**: use the open slot; if none, push one evergreen piece, not a date-bound one.
- **A guest or collaborator cancels**: use the reserve episode or piece and rebook; don't record a rushed replacement.
- **The pillars stop fitting**: retire or merge one at the monthly review rather than adding a sixth.

## Review cadence

- **Weekly (15 minutes)**: what shipped, what slipped and why, buffer count, next two weeks' dependencies (guests, data, approvals).
- **Monthly**: pillar balance, which pieces worked (by the goal's metric, not vanity counts), refill ideas, adjust next month's slots.
- **Quarterly or per season / per manuscript milestone**: cadence still sustainable? Pillars still right? Re-measure hours per piece and recompute cadence.

## Common failure modes

- Cadence set from ambition instead of measured hours.
- Only publish dates on the calendar; research and draft dates missing, so every piece starts late.
- No buffer, so the first sick week breaks the streak.
- Every slot filled, nothing left for timely pieces.
- Repurposing as an afterthought, so derivatives never happen.
- Pillars so broad that any idea fits, or so many that none builds an audience.
- Podcast guests booked a week out; book chapters scheduled with no revision time.
- A calendar never compared against what actually shipped.
- Planning more channels than the person can feed.

## Done means

- A stated cadence per channel, with the capacity math behind it and labeled estimates.
- Three to five pillars with descriptions and target shares.
- A dated table covering the planning horizon, with upstream stage dates for every piece and external dependencies named.
- Key dates marked; at least one flexible slot per month or so.
- A buffer rule (how many pieces finished ahead) and what happens when it's breached.
- Repurposing chains for each flagship, slotted.
- Weekly and monthly review steps written into the plan.
- The implied weekly hours fit the person's capacity.
